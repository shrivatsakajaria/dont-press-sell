# Start screen: brief (for SK, or to paste into another model)

## The game in one paragraph
A free phone game, opened from a link in LinkedIn, X or a group chat, usually inside the app's own browser. It has about five seconds to win a stranger's attention. You get $10,000 of play money in the US stock market, dropped in on the day a real historical fall has just begun (the market is already about 7% below its high). You are not told which episode it is: half are real crashes (1929, 1987, 2000, 2008, 2020, 2022), half are false alarms that blew over within weeks or months. The days run by themselves. The screen is a newspaper front page: the headline is that day's real news, and the paper turns red on the worst days. There is one button. It always shows a live offer: "Sell for $8,782". Press it and you are in cash; it then reads "Buy back now: $1,158 ahead" and counts up or down every day. The game always runs until the market is back above its old high, then prints a front page about you ("Local investor sells three days before the bottom") and reveals which crash it was. A game lasts about two minutes.

## How the scoring actually works (this is the part every draft so far has got wrong)
- **Doing nothing is a draw.** Every run ends with the market back above its old high, so someone who never touches the button always finishes about 7% up. That is the benchmark: the result says "$2,300 behind someone who did nothing" or "$1,640 ahead of someone who did nothing".
- **You cannot win without pressing sell.** The only way to beat a draw is to sell, then buy back in lower than you sold.
- **Pressing sell is also the only way to lose.** Sell and never buy back, or buy back higher, and you finish behind.
- **You don't know whether it's a crash or a false alarm.** In a crash, selling early and buying near the bottom wins big. In a false alarm the market turns straight back up, and anyone who sold is punished.

So the name "Don't Press Sell" is ironic. It is the safe advice, and it guarantees a draw. The whole game is the pull between the safe draw and the temptation of the button, which is both the escape and the only road to a win. That is the gambling structure: a guaranteed small outcome against a bet you can't fully read. The closest TV analogy is Deal or No Deal: the button is the banker's offer, made fresh every day.

## Who reads the start screen
Strangers on a phone, mid-scroll, half paying attention. Some know markets well; many know nothing about them (they don't know what "buy back" means). They need, in about five seconds:
1. It's play money (several testers worried it might be real).
2. What's happening: the market is falling, and nobody knows how far.
3. What winning is. This is the piece the earlier drafts fudged.
4. A pull: a reason to press Start now rather than scroll on.

## Constraints
Three short lines, then one "Start" button. Plain British English, sentence case, no exclamation marks, no emoji. No made-up statistics, and nothing that reads as financial advice. The look is a newspaper front page, so the start text can be written as the paper's own headline and standfirst.

## What testers said so far (10-person simulated panel, three rounds)
- Best-liked phrases: "It's play money, but it won't feel like it" (honest and a dare); "Every instinct will tell you to sell"; "Real crash or false alarm? You won't know"; "Most money wins"; "Bet you press it" (most screenshot-able, but doesn't say how to win).
- Disliked: history-lesson openings, bank-app tone, "diamond hands / paper hands" (jargon to most), anything without a clear win condition, and "time it right" (sounds like advice).

## My candidate, built on the real scoring
> You have $10,000 of play money. The market is falling.
> Sit tight and it all comes back. That's a draw.
> To win, sell before the bottom and buy back cheaper. If you can tell where it is.

Why: it tells the truth about the game in plain words, and turns "doing nothing" from a dull benchmark into a draw people want to beat. It also explains the button without jargon and leaves the fear in place: you don't know where the bottom is, or whether there's one at all.

Variant written as the newspaper's own front page:
> Headline: **Markets fall. You have $10,000 in them.**
> Standfirst: It's play money. Sit tight and it comes back: that's a draw. Sell and buy back cheaper to win.
